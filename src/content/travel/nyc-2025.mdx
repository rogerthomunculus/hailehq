### Why this one is structured differently

Our other guides are trips — a start date, an end date, a route. New York isn't that for us. We've been several times, from different hotels, in different seasons, and what's actually useful isn't a day-by-day of any one visit. It's the accumulated list: the udon place we'd go back to tomorrow, the pizza the kids ask for by name, the walk that turned out to be a slog.

So this is organised the way you'd actually use it on a phone, standing on a corner deciding where to eat. Food is grouped by neighbourhood, because in New York the question is never "what's the best X in the city," it's "what's good within ten minutes of where I'm standing." Everything else is grouped by what kind of day it is — indoor, outdoor, or wandering.

Every entry has an address and a map link. The opinions are ours.

### The one structural thing to get right

Pick your neighbourhood before you pick your hotel. In a city this dense, the twenty minutes you save not travelling to dinner is worth more than a nicer room, and with kids that twenty minutes is the difference between a good night and a meltdown. All three of the hotels below were fine rooms. Only two of them were in the right place.

<Note type="win" label="Go if">
  You want a trip where the kids are genuinely entertained without you driving anywhere, and where a
  single subway ride gets you from dinosaurs to dumplings. NYC rewards families who are willing to
  walk and eat.
</Note>

<Note type="warn" label="Think harder if">
  You need naps on a schedule, hate crowds, or want a trip that's restful. This city is loud, dense,
  and physically demanding — we routinely put in eight or nine miles a day on foot with kids in tow.
</Note>

### MetroCard is gone. It's OMNY now.

MetroCard was retired in January 2026. Every fare now runs through OMNY, the contactless tap-to-pay system. There is no card to buy in advance and no machine to queue at. You tap:

- a contactless credit or debit card,
- a phone or watch with that card in its wallet, or
- a physical OMNY card, sold from vending machines in every subway station and at roughly 2,700 corner shops if you'd rather not use a bank card.

You tap on entry only. There's no tap-out, no zones, and no distance pricing — one flat fare gets you from the Bronx to Coney Island.

### What it costs, and the cap that makes it cheap

The fare is $3 for most riders on the subway and local buses; express buses are $7.25. The part worth understanding is the weekly cap:

Once you've paid for 12 trips within a rolling seven-day period on the same card or device, every additional ride that week is free — $35 is the most you'll pay. The seven days start from your first tap, not from Monday.

There's no signup and no pass to pre-purchase. It's the old 7-day Unlimited without the upfront gamble. The one rule that matters: **tap with the exact same card or device every single time.** If you tap your phone on Monday and your physical Amex on Tuesday, OMNY counts them as two different riders and neither one reaches the cap. Pick one payment method per person on day one and stick to it for the whole trip.

### Kids and the turnstile

Up to three children under 44 inches tall ride free on the subway and local buses when they're with a fare-paying adult. Forty-four inches is roughly an average five-year-old, and it's measured by height, not age — a tall four-year-old pays, a short six-year-old doesn't. Nobody checks ID. The kid goes under the turnstile with you, or through the wide accessible gate if you've got a stroller.

Above that height, kids pay the full $3 adult fare. There's no child discount on the subway. (There _is_ a $1 Family Fare, but it applies to Metro-North and the LIRR — the commuter railroads — not to the subway, so it won't help you inside the city.)

### Reading the system without an app fighting you

Two things unlock the map:

**Local vs. express.** Most lines run both. Local trains stop everywhere; express trains skip most stations. On the platform signage and the maps, express stops are the white circles, locals are the small black dots. Getting on an express by accident is the classic tourist error — you'll sail past your stop and surface fifteen blocks north. If you're only going a few stops, take the local.

**Uptown vs. downtown.** Many stations have separate street entrances for each direction with no crossover underneath. The stair railing tells you: "Uptown & The Bronx" or "Downtown & Brooklyn." Read it before you go down, because if you get it wrong you'll tap in, realise, and have to exit and pay again on the other side.

Use Google Maps or Citymapper for routing — both have live service data, which matters because weekend track work reroutes lines constantly and the map on the wall won't reflect it.

### From the airports

- **JFK** — AirTrain to Jamaica or Howard Beach, then the subway ($3 fare plus the AirTrain charge). Cheap, and about an hour to Midtown. With luggage and tired kids, it's a lot of stairs.
- **LaGuardia** — no rail link. Taxi, rideshare, or the M60/Q70 bus. Closest to Manhattan, most exposed to traffic.
- **Newark** — AirTrain to Newark Liberty station, then NJ Transit into Penn Station. Reliable, and often the least painful with bags.

On arrival with kids and suitcases, we've taken a car from the airport every time and felt fine about it. It's the one leg where the subway isn't worth the savings. After that, the subway does everything.

### Uber Car Seat — real, but read the fine print

New York is one of a handful of US cities where you can book a ride that arrives with a car seat already installed, which is genuinely why we've travelled to NYC without lugging one through an airport. It works. Two caveats worth knowing before you count on it:

Each Uber Car Seat trip includes **only one car seat**. If you need a second, you bring it yourself. There's a $10 surcharge, the rides are reservation-only, and the seat is a Nuna RAVA convertible — rear- or forward-facing, rated for children 5 to 65 lbs.

So for one small child it's excellent. For two kids who both still need seats, it solves half your problem. Lyft runs a similar Car Seat Mode in NYC with a forward-facing seat.

Also worth knowing, and slightly uncomfortable: New York's child restraint law exempts taxis and liveries, so yellow cabs and TLC-licensed cars can legally carry a child with no seat at all. Legal isn't the same as safe, and it's your call — but it means nobody will stop you, and nobody will provide one either unless you've booked it.

<Note label="The taxi trick nobody mentions">
  Yellow cabs are metered and often *cheaper* than surge-priced rideshare in Midtown at rush hour,
  and you don't wait for a car to find you. Hail one, tap the card reader, done. Worth remembering
  when the app quotes you something absurd.
</Note>

<Note type="warn" label="The Canal Street caveat">
  SoHo Grand's location is great in three directions and not in the fourth. Head the wrong way and
  you're on a rough stretch of Canal Street with a significant homeless population and a lot of
  street hustle. It's not dangerous so much as unpleasant and not what you want to walk your kids
  through at night. Know which way you're going before you leave the lobby.
</Note>

### What we'd tell someone choosing

If you want food to be the trip, stay downtown — SoHo Grand or anywhere in that radius. If you want the easiest possible logistics with kids, stay at Union Square: you're on top of the subway, you're near the park, you have a farmers' market, and you can reach both uptown museums and downtown food in about fifteen minutes. Midtown East we'd skip.

<Section
  id="kids"
  num="11"
  title="Doing NYC with kids"
  subtitle="The practical stuff — bathrooms, pacing, and the things that made the difference between a good day and a bad one."
>

<div class="measure">

### Bathrooms are the whole game

This is the single most important logistical thing about New York with children, and nobody puts it first. **Always know where the next bathroom is.** Public restrooms are scarce, and most restaurants, cafés, and shops will expect you to be a customer — you may well have to buy something to use one.

The reliable ones, worth memorising:

- **Museums** — always, and usually clean. The Met and AMNH both have them on every floor.
- **Department stores and big chains** — Macy's, Nordstrom, and the like. Starbucks is inconsistent now; don't count on it.
- **Food halls** — Chelsea Market, Turnstyle, Time Out Market. Reliable and free.
- **Hotel lobbies** — walk in like you're staying there. This works more often than it should.
- **Grand Central and Penn Station** — proper public facilities.
- **Central Park playgrounds** — several of the larger ones have restroom buildings, including Heckscher and Ancient.

Our rule ended up being: use the bathroom whenever you're already in a place that has one, whether or not anyone says they need to. Don't wait for the request.

### Pace, and the mileage nobody warns you about

We routinely walked eight or nine miles a day here without planning to. The city rewards walking, and the distances look shorter on a map than they feel on the pavement — twenty blocks is a mile, and Manhattan is twenty blocks tall about eleven times over.

What worked: one big thing per day, not two. A museum in the morning, food, then something outdoors and unstructured — a playground, a park, a market. The unstructured half is what makes the structured half survivable.

### Strollers and the subway

Only about a quarter of subway stations have elevators, so plan on carrying the stroller down stairs at some point. Two adults makes this trivial and one adult makes it hard. A compact, one-hand-fold stroller is worth much more here than a comfortable one.

At the turnstile, use the wide accessible gate at the side rather than trying to fold and squeeze through. You still tap; the gate opens.

### Eating out with kids, honestly

Downtown New York's best restaurants are frequently small, loud, trendy rooms that are not designed around children. We've made those work, but the trick is timing rather than persuasion: go at opening, or go at lunch. A 5:30pm dinner at a hip place is an entirely different experience from an 8pm one, and nobody minds you at 5:30.

The genuinely kid-easy formats here are counter service and food halls — Joe's, Los Tacos, Turnstyle, Chelsea Market, Time Out Market. Rotate those in between the sit-down meals.

### A few things we're glad we did

- **Kept a "pizza in reserve."** Joe's is open until 3am. Knowing there was a guaranteed acceptable meal available at any hour took the pressure off every experimental dinner.
- **Let the kids pick one thing a day.** Usually a dessert. It bought a lot of goodwill on the walking.
- **Used the hotel as a mid-afternoon base.** If you're staying centrally, going back for an hour is not the wasted time it feels like.
- **Booked the timed-entry things early.** CAMP, the Butterfly Vivarium, Mercer Labs — all sell specific slots, and the good ones go.

<Section id="verdict" num="13" title="The verdict" subtitle="After several trips.">

<div class="measure">

New York is the easiest great city we take our kids to. There's no passport, no language gap, no car to rent or park, and the subway means a five-year-old can get from dinosaurs to dumplings in twenty minutes without anyone driving. The trade-off is that it asks a lot physically — it's loud, dense, and you will walk further than you mean to every single day.

The thing we'd say to someone going for the first time with children: pick a neighbourhood and let it do the work. Our best trips have been the ones where we stayed at Union Square or downtown and treated the surrounding fifteen minutes as the trip, with occasional expeditions uptown for a museum. Our worst was the one where we stayed somewhere convenient for offices.

If we could only keep five things off this whole list: Raku, the Natural History Museum's bug hall, wandering Chinatown, the High Line into Chelsea Market, and a slice at Joe's at an hour no restaurant should be open. Everything else is a bonus.

We'll be back, and this list will keep growing.

</div>
