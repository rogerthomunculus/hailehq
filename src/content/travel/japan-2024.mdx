## About Japan

One month, five base camps, eight cities. This guide is a real account of that trip — Tokyo (twice, bookending the month), Osaka as the ten-night hub for Kyoto/Nara/Kobe day trips, Hiroshima, and Hakone — plus everything a first-timer needs that isn't specific to any one stop: entry requirements, money, etiquette, language, and how the whole transit system actually works. It's written to be useful whether you're copying the itinerary outright or just borrowing the parts that fit your own trip.

### The Don't-Skip List

A month gives you room to be picky. If your trip is shorter than ours, these are the five things across the whole month we'd tell you not to cut.

#### Miyajima Island

- Don't skip this. The day trip from Hiroshima was one of the best of the whole month

#### 🐋 Kobe Suma Sea World

- Don't skip this, even with the 90-minute train ride. See the Kobe section — the aquarium beyond the orcas was as good as the orcas themselves

#### The Insect Expo at Tokyo Skytree

- Don't skip this if it's running when you visit — holding the beetles was a genuine highlight for the kids, not just a filler add-on to the observation deck ticket

#### teamLab (Borderless or Planets)

- Don't skip this. The best immersive art experiences we've tried anywhere — see the Tokyo section for the full take

#### Yukata dress-up & photo shoot

- Don't skip this. Worth doing once, even with the imperfect photos — see Yumeyakata Kimono in the Kyoto section

## Entry Requirements

Good news for US travelers: this is genuinely simple. Here's exactly what's required, as of 2026.

### Do you need a visa?

- No — US citizens are visa-exempt for tourism, short business trips, and visiting family/friends, for stays up to 90 days
- This covers over 70 countries total, including the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and most of the EU
- Visa-free entry doesn't guarantee admission — the immigration officer at the airport makes the final call, so have your return ticket and accommodation details ready if asked

### Passport

- Japan officially only requires your passport to be valid for the duration of your stay — no 6-month rule like some countries
- That said, many airlines enforce their own 6-month-validity policy and can deny boarding over it, so check with your carrier and renew early if you're close to that window
- You need at least one fully blank page for the entry stamp
- A damaged, water-stained, or peeling passport can cause problems at immigration — get a new one if yours is rough

### Before you land

- Register on [Visit Japan Web](https://vjw-lp.digital.go.jp/en/) ahead of time — it generates the immigration and customs QR codes that speed up the airport process significantly (see Before You Go)
- Have proof of onward/return travel — airlines can ask before they'll issue a boarding pass
- Travel insurance isn't legally required for entry, but strongly worth having given how expensive Japanese healthcare can be out of pocket

### Once you're there

- Visa-exempt entry grants "Temporary Visitor" status for up to 90 days, starting the day you actually land — not the day you booked
- This status doesn't allow paid work or long-term study
- A US green card (permanent residency) does **not** grant visa-free entry on its own — it depends on your actual citizenship, not your US residency status

## Money & Payments

Japan has a reputation as cash-only. That's outdated, but not entirely wrong — the truth is a mix, and the easiest approach is cards for most things, cash as a buffer, IC card for everything in between.

### The real picture

- Card acceptance has improved a lot — hotels, department stores, chain restaurants, and convenience stores all take Visa and Mastercard reliably
- What still needs cash: small ramen counters, neighborhood izakaya, shrine and temple donation boxes, coin lockers, and some rural buses and taxis
- Carry roughly ¥15,000–20,000 per person at any given time — enough to never be caught out, not so much you're hauling a stack of bills
- Visa and Mastercard are accepted almost everywhere that takes cards at all. JCB is a solid backup. American Express and Diners are noticeably patchier outside big chains

### IC cards (Suica / Pasmo)

- Beyond train gates, these tap-to-pay at convenience stores, vending machines, station kiosks, and a long list of chain shops — genuinely the fastest way to pay for anything small
- If you have a recent iPhone or an NFC Android phone, you can add a digital Suica or Pasmo to your phone's wallet in a few minutes and top it up with a foreign card — no need to track down a physical card or the machine that sells them
- See the Welcome Suica Card entry in Before You Go for the kids'-fare version we actually used

### ATMs

- **7-Eleven (Seven Bank) ATMs are the most reliable for foreign cards** — English menu, accepts Visa, Mastercard, Maestro, Cirrus, and runs 24/7. This matches what worked for us — see Money in Before You Go
- Lawson and FamilyMart ATMs also take foreign cards, with a somewhat lower success rate — if one machine declines you, try a different chain before assuming your card doesn't work here at all
- Most bank ATMs (not the convenience-store ones) do **not** reliably take foreign cards
- Expect a foreign-transaction fee from your own bank on top of whatever the Japanese ATM charges

### Tax-free shopping

- Non-resident tourists can buy goods tax-free (skip the 10% consumption tax) at any store displaying a "Tax Free" sign — department stores, electronics chains like Bic Camera and Yodobashi, drugstores, and many souvenir shops
- Minimum purchase is ¥5,000 (before tax) in a single store on a single day. Bring your passport — it's required at checkout
- Consumables (food, cosmetics) bought tax-free must leave the country unopened
- The system is changing from Nov 1, 2026: instead of skipping the tax at checkout, you'll pay the full tax-inclusive price and apply for a refund before departure. If you're traveling after that date, plan for the refund process rather than an instant discount

### Tipping

- There is no tipping culture in Japan — not at restaurants, not for taxis, not for hotel staff or tour guides
- Leaving money on the table doesn't read as generous, it reads as confusing — staff have been known to chase travelers down to return what they assume was forgotten change
- The one common exception is a small, folded cash gift in an envelope for a private tour guide or ryokan housekeeper — never handed over loose

## Etiquette Basics

Nobody will yell at you for getting these wrong — Japanese culture is famously forgiving of tourist mistakes. But knowing them ahead of time means you spend less energy watching what everyone else is doing.

### On trains

- No phone calls, ever. Put your phone on silent (called "manner mode" locally)
- Keep conversation quiet — trains are treated as shared quiet space, not a place to chat loudly
- Eating and drinking is frowned on on local/commuter trains, but completely normal on the Shinkansen and other long-distance reserved trains — see Riding the Shinkansen
- Queue in the marked lines on the platform and let people off before boarding

### Shoes & indoor spaces

- Remove your shoes at the entrance of homes, ryokan, some restaurants, and temple interiors — look for a shoe rack or a step up onto a different floor level as the cue
- Slippers are usually provided; there's often a _separate_ pair just for the toilet — swap into those at the bathroom door and swap back out before rejoining the room. Forgetting this is the classic tourist slip-up
- Wear socks without holes on days you know you'll be taking shoes off — you will end up doing this more than you'd expect

### Trash

- Public trash cans are genuinely rare, yet the streets stay remarkably clean — the local habit is to carry your trash until you're back at a hotel or find a konbini bin
- Keep a small bag in your day pack for wrappers and empty bottles

### Shrines & temples

- Purify your hands (and traditionally your mouth) at the water basin (_temizuya_) near the entrance before approaching
- Shrine prayer: toss a coin in the offering box, bow twice, clap twice, make your silent prayer, bow once more
- Temple prayer is quieter — palms together, no clapping
- Photography is often restricted inside main halls — look for signage or just watch what other visitors are doing
- Stay quiet and respectful inside, even though the grounds outside are often lively

### Onsen & public baths

- Wash thoroughly at the shower stations **before** entering the bath — this isn't optional, it's the whole point of the ritual
- No swimsuits in a traditional public onsen; a private in-room onsen (like the one we used in Hakone) is a different story and doesn't carry this rule the same way
- Long hair gets tied up so it doesn't touch the water
- Tattoos are traditionally banned at many onsen due to old associations with organized crime, though this is loosening — some venues allow a cover patch, others offer tattoo-friendly hours, and private baths sidestep the issue entirely. Worth checking a venue's English website in advance if this applies to you

### Dining

- Don't stand chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice, and don't pass food chopstick-to-chopstick — both echo funeral rituals and are the two genuine taboos worth knowing
- Slurping noodles is totally fine, even encouraged
- At the register, place your money in the small tray provided rather than handing it directly to the cashier
- No tipping — see Money & Payments

## Language & Communication

You don't need fluency. You need about a dozen words used consistently, plus a translation app for everything else. Major cities and tourist sites have English signage; smaller towns and family-run spots often don't.

### The essentials

- **Sumimasen** (すみません) — excuse me / sorry / how to get someone's attention. The single most useful word in the language for a traveler
- **Arigatou gozaimasu** (ありがとうございます) — thank you. "Arigatou" alone is a fine, slightly more casual version
- **Onegaishimasu** (お願いします) — please / a polite request, tack it onto almost anything
- **Kudasai** (ください) — please give me — use with an item: "kore o kudasai" (this, please)
- **Ikura desu ka?** (いくらですか) — how much is it?
- **Eigo no menyuu wa arimasu ka?** (英語のメニューはありますか) — do you have an English menu?
- **Toire wa doko desu ka?** (トイレはどこですか) — where is the bathroom?
- **Hai** / **Iie** — yes / no

### Apps that carry the rest

- Google Translate's camera mode — point it at a menu, package, or sign and it overlays a live translation. We used this constantly, see Navigation in Before You Go
- Google Maps' station-level accuracy for trains is genuinely reliable — trust it down to the platform number
- It's not considered rude to speak English — most people in tourist areas and businesses have at least basic English. The courtesy is to open with a few Japanese words first, then switch

### What to expect

- English signage is strong at major stations, tourist sites, and chain restaurants
- It thins out fast in smaller towns, family-run restaurants, and rural areas — this is where the translation app earns its keep
- You do not need to learn hiragana, katakana, or kanji for a trip like this — station names and menus almost always have a romanized version alongside

## Konbini

You'll see 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, and Lawson signs constantly throughout this guide — they came up in nearly every city. They deserve their own explanation, because "convenience store" undersells what they actually do.

### Not what the name suggests

- A konbini is a 24/7 institution — genuinely more like a bank branch, post office, and quality-controlled restaurant sharing one small footprint than the gas-station shelf the English term implies
- 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, and Lawson are the three you'll actually see; they're interchangeable for daily use, each with a couple of signature items — 7-Eleven's egg sandwich, FamilyMart's famichiki fried chicken, Lawson's Karaage-kun

### What you can actually do there

- Withdraw cash — 7-Eleven's Seven Bank ATM is the most reliable for foreign cards (see Money & Payments)
- Pay utility, phone, and tax bills in cash at the register — just hand over the paper bill with its barcode
- Print or scan documents — every konbini has a multifunction printer, useful for a boarding pass or a form you need on paper
- Pick up tickets — many event, concert, and theme-park tickets in Japan are sold through konbini kiosks (Loppi at Lawson, FamiPort at FamilyMart) rather than sent directly online. Have your reservation code ready
- Ship a package — Yamato and Sagawa both have drop-off points at most locations, usually next-day delivery nationwide

### Why it matters for families specifically

- Genuinely good, cheap hot food at any hour — onigiri, fried chicken, bento boxes — which makes it a reliable fallback when kids won't eat the adventurous option. See the "Have backups" card in With Kids
- Air conditioning strong enough to double as a heat-break stop — see Beating the Heat
- Both IC cards and credit cards work at the register for anything you're buying (though bill payment specifically is usually cash-only)

### Trains, Luggage & Passes

The part of the trip that takes the most planning and gets written about the least. With five bags, two carry-ons, and two strollers, moving between cities was the single hardest logistical problem of the month.

#### The oversized luggage rule — read this first

_Tokaido, Sanyo, Kyushu & Nishi-Kyushu Shinkansen_

- Since May 2020, any bag whose three dimensions add up to more than 160 cm needs a reserved "seat with oversized baggage area." Boarding without one is a ¥1,000 penalty per bag
- The reservation itself is free — you just have to make it. Most standard large check-in suitcases sit right at 157–158 cm, so they squeak under; anything larger does not
- Nothing over 250 cm total is allowed on board at all
- Strollers, sports gear, and instruments are exempt from the rule, though you can still reserve space for them
- Measure with wheels and handles included — there are measuring frames near the Shinkansen gates
- **This is the real reason luggage forwarding is worth $20 a bag.** Forwarding the big suitcases ahead means you board with only what fits overhead, and skip the reservation problem entirely

#### The inter-city legs we actually rode

- Tokyo → Shin-Osaka: about 2.5 hrs on the Nozomi, 3 hrs on the Hikari. Trains leave roughly every 15 minutes
- Shin-Osaka → Hiroshima: about 1.5 hrs on the Nozomi
- Hiroshima → Tokyo direct: just under 4 hrs on the Nozomi; about 5 hrs if you transfer at Shin-Osaka instead
- For Hakone: take the Shinkansen to Odawara, then the Hakone Tozan Railway in. From Tokyo Station that's roughly 50 minutes total
- Buy at a manned counter rather than in an app — see the Smart EX warning in Before You Go

#### Day trips from the Osaka base

- Kyoto — about 15 min by Shinkansen, or ~30 min on regular lines
- Nara — roughly 45 min
- Kobe (Suma Sea World) — about 1.5 hrs
- All are comfortable same-day round trips, which is what made the 10-night Osaka stay work

#### Do you need a JR Pass?

- Probably not, and this surprises people. After the 2023 price hike the nationwide pass runs ¥50,000 for 7 days / ¥80,000 for 14
- It only breaks even if you're stringing several long-distance legs into a short window. A Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka loop, or a single long round trip, is cheaper on individual tickets
- The pass also doesn't cover the fastest Nozomi and Mizuho trains without a surcharge — you'd be on the slower Hikari or transferring at Shin-Osaka
- For a base-camp itinerary like ours, with long stays and only four inter-city moves, point-to-point tickets won easily
- Kids 6–11 ride at half fare; under 6 ride free on a lap. Regional passes (JR West, Kansai Wide) are often the better buy if you want a pass at all

#### Hakone Free Pass — the one pass that did pay off

- Covers the Tozan railway, cable car, ropeway, Lake Ashi pirate cruise, and designated buses, plus discounts at 70+ local museums and onsen
- Riding the full Hakone loop à la carte runs past ¥8,000, so the pass clears its own cost in a single day
- Sold in 2-day and 3-day versions; buying from Odawara is cheaper but excludes the Tokyo connection
- Watch out: other bus companies operate in the area and don't accept it — this caused us real confusion
- The Romancecar limited express to/from Shinjuku is _not_ included; it's a ~¥1,200 seat surcharge on top

## Riding the Shinkansen

It looks intimidating from the outside and turns out to be one of the easiest things about travelling in Japan. Here's the whole process, in order, including the two parts that actually trip people up.

### 1 · Buy the ticket

- Go to a manned ticket counter (_midori no madoguchi_) rather than an app. We tried Smart EX, it crashed mid-purchase, and the refund took days
- Tell them your destination, date, rough time, and how many adults and children. They'll print everything
- Ask for a **reserved seat** (指定席 _shiteiseki_) if you're travelling as a family — unreserved cars are first-come, first-served and you may end up standing or split up
- This is the moment to also ask for an oversized-baggage seat if any bag exceeds 160 cm total — see Getting Around

### 2 · Understand what you're holding

- A Shinkansen fare is actually two tickets: a **base fare ticket** and a **limited express supplement**. Sometimes they're combined onto one slip, sometimes they're two separate slips
- If you have two slips, **insert them into the gate together, stacked**. This is the single most common point of confusion
- Your ticket lists: train name and number, departure time, car number, and seat (row + letter, like an airplane)

### 3 · Through the gates

- Shinkansen gates are separate from the local JR gates and marked in blue. At Tokyo Station, check the line name too — Tokaido and Tohoku Shinkansen have different gate areas entirely
- Insert your ticket(s), then **take them back out the other side**. You need them again to exit at your destination
- If you entered the station through a local gate first, you'll hit a second set of transfer gates — same procedure

### 4 · Find your car on the platform

- Check the electronic departure board for your train name and platform
- Platforms are painted with car numbers and door positions. Find _your_ car number and stand at that marker — the train stops exactly there, every time
- Unreserved cars are usually 1–3; look for 自由席 on the signage
- Arrive 10–15 minutes early. Platforms are long, and with kids and bags you want the buffer

### 5 · On board

- Let arriving passengers off first, then board. Dwell time at each station is short — sometimes under two minutes
- Overhead racks are about 42 cm deep and swallow a medium roller bag or large backpack easily
- Eating and drinking is completely normal here (unlike on local trains). Fold-down tray tables are built for it
- Buy your _ekiben_ (station bento) and drinks **before boarding** — the trolley cart service was discontinued on most Tokaido routes in 2023
- Seats rotate to face each other, which is worth knowing with a family of four — ask staff or do it yourself when the car is empty

### 6 · Getting off

- Start gathering your things a few minutes out — the stop is brief
- At the Shinkansen exit gate, insert your ticket(s) again. If you inserted two, **one comes back out** — take it
- You're still inside the JR station at that point. Insert that last ticket at the regular gate to actually exit

### Worth knowing

- Nozomi and Mizuho are the fastest services; Hikari and Sakura stop more; Kodama stops everywhere
- Trains are punctual to the minute. If it says 10:47, it leaves at 10:47
- Kids under 6 ride free on a lap; 6–11 pay half fare
- Free Wi-Fi is available, and most cars have power outlets
- Mount Fuji appears on the **right side** heading Tokyo → Osaka, and the left heading back — worth requesting seat E when you book

## Packing

Pack to manage heat, not just to dress for it. And pack lighter than feels sensible — you can buy almost anything on the ground, and every extra bag is one more thing to haul through a station.

### Clothing

- Everything light, breathable, and quick-drying. You will sweat through cotton in about twenty minutes
- Linen, or moisture-wicking synthetics. Uniqlo AIRism is what locals actually wear and it's worth buying a few once you land
- More shirts than you think — plan on changing mid-day on the worst days
- Skip jeans entirely
- One light layer for indoors: air conditioning on trains and in malls runs genuinely cold
- Something covering shoulders and knees for temple visits
- Comfortable walking shoes, and slip-ons help — you'll take shoes off more than you expect

### Bring from home

- A rechargeable handheld or neck fan (or buy at Daiso/Don Quijote for a few hundred yen)
- Cooling towels — they pack flat and work well in humidity
- Reusable water bottles, plus holders for the kids so they carry their own
- A folding umbrella that works for both sun and sudden downpours
- Any prescription medication, plus your prescriptions on paper
- A power bank — you'll be on Google Maps all day

### Don't bother packing

- Shampoo, conditioner, body wash, toothbrushes, razors — every hotel we stayed at provided them
- Hair straighteners or curling irons. At 80% humidity it's a losing battle
- Bulk diapers, wipes, or formula — drugstores and Akachan Honpo have everything
- Excessive clothing if your hotel has coin laundry. Most do, and it's the single best way to pack light for a month

### Buy once you're there

- Biore cooling wipes and cooling body spray — the best ¥400 you'll spend
- A sun umbrella (日傘) from Don Quijote; UV-blocking ones genuinely drop the temperature around you
- Cooling neck rings — gel tubes that refreeze quickly. We didn't try these and regretted it
- Salt tablets and Pocari Sweat from any konbini
- Kids' clothes at Uniqlo, Muji, or Nishimatsuya. Sizes are in centimeters (90 ≈ 1.5–2 years)

### Luggage strategy

- **Nest your suitcases before you leave home.** We traveled with 6 bags total, packed as two sets of nesting suitcases — each set was two progressively smaller cases packed inside the largest one. Coming home, we unnested them and had all that extra capacity ready-made for souvenirs and gifts, instead of trying to cram everything into bags that were already full
- Keep every bag under 160 cm in total dimensions if you can, so the Shinkansen oversized rule never applies to you
- Umbrella strollers over full-size — lighter, one-hand fold, and station elevators aren't always close
- Leave real room for what you're bringing home. We came back with knives from Kappabashi, gashapon, and arcade prizes
- Budget ~$20 per bag per leg for luggage forwarding and use it — it's the difference between a pleasant travel day and a miserable one

## Beating the Heat

July in Japan is roughly 30–35°C with humidity above 75%, which means the "real feel" sits much higher. This was the single biggest constraint on how much we could do in a day, and it's the thing most first-timers underestimate.

### Structure the day around it

- Big outdoor thing in the morning, indoor thing in the afternoon. Museums, aquariums, arcades, and malls are all air conditioned
- Plan a konbini or café stop roughly every 20–30 minutes of walking. This sounds excessive and it isn't
- Popsicle and shaved-ice breaks were our most reliable reset with the kids — and they're genuinely everywhere
- Go back to the hotel mid-day and change clothes if you can. It resets the whole afternoon

### Hydration is not optional

- Plain water alone won't cut it — you lose salt through sweat all day
- **Pocari Sweat** is the standard: a mild, non-carbonated electrolyte drink sold in every vending machine for about ¥150. Aquarius is the Coca-Cola equivalent
- Alternate with plain water or barley tea (_mugicha_) so you're not drinking sugar all day
- Salt tablets and salt candies from any konbini
- Aim for 2 litres a day minimum, more if you're walking a lot. There are roughly five million vending machines in Japan — you're never far from a cold drink

### The gear that works

- **Cooling wipes** (Biore, Gatsby) — menthol sheets that wipe off sweat and genuinely cool your skin. ¥300–500 at any konbini
- **Cooling body spray** — same idea, aerosol version
- **Neck fan or handheld fan** — get a decent rechargeable one; cheap ones are useless
- **Cooling neck ring** — a gel tube you refreeze overnight. The one thing we didn't try and wish we had
- **Sun umbrella** — not just for women; UV-blocking parasols are standard here and lower the temperature around you noticeably
- A small towel. Everyone carries one, and you'll understand why by day two

### Know the warning signs

*heatstroke — 熱中症 *netchūshō\*\*

- Early: dizziness on standing, muscle cramps, heavy sweating. Get into air conditioning and drink an electrolyte drink immediately
- Worse: headache, nausea, vomiting, extreme fatigue. Don't try to "walk it off" back to the hotel
- Cool the neck, armpits, and wrists with ice or a cold drink held against the skin
- Emergency number in Japan is **119** and the ambulance is free
- Kids overheat faster than adults and are worse at reporting it — check in on them actively rather than waiting for complaints

## With Kids

Japan is one of the easiest countries in the world to travel with young children, but a few things are worth knowing before you're standing in a station holding a folded stroller and a tired six-year-old.

### Strollers and stations

- Most major stations have elevators, but they're often at the far edge of the station rather than near your platform — a "4-minute transfer" can become 12 with a stroller
- Set Google Maps to the wheelchair-accessible route option; it routes you via elevators
- Some carriages are marked as stroller/wheelchair spaces — look for the pictogram on the platform floor
- Bring the lightest one-hand-fold stroller you own. We travelled with two umbrella strollers and never regretted it
- Avoid 7:30–9:30 AM and 5–7 PM entirely if you can

### Fares for children

- Under 6: free on trains and buses (carry them through the gate)
- 6–11: half fare — this is what the Welcome Suica card is for
- 12 and up: full adult fare
- Letting a kid carry and tap their own IC card turns transit into part of the fun rather than a chore

### Baby and family facilities

- Nursing and changing rooms are in nearly every station, department store, and mall — follow the baby-and-bottle icon
- They're private, spotless, and often have hot-water dispensers for formula
- The free Mamamap / Mama Papa app finds the nearest one
- Public restrooms frequently have toddler seats built into the stall

### Supplies on the ground

- Don't overpack diapers or formula — Akachan Honpo, Nishimatsuya, and any drugstore stock everything
- Kids' clothing sizes are in centimeters (size 90 ≈ 1.5–2 years); Uniqlo and Muji are everywhere
- Amazon Japan Prime covers the gaps, as long as you're staying put for a few days

### Have backups

- The real key to eating in Japan with kids: always have a fallback for when they don't want the adventurous option
- Our reliable trio was Hanamaru Udon, FamilyMart fried chicken, and 7-Eleven — all consistently kept the kids happy and well fed on the days nothing else landed
- Knowing these were always nearby took the pressure off every other meal decision

### Pacing

- One "big" attraction per day, with food and cooling breaks between, was our ceiling in July heat
- Build in slow days deliberately — Shukkeien Garden and a mall visit like Sunshine City both served that purpose well
- Arcades (Round1 especially) are the reliable afternoon reset when everyone's done with sightseeing
- Eating and drinking on local trains is discouraged; save snacks for the Shinkansen, where it's expected
