### A mining town that turned into a ski town

Park City started as a silver camp after ore was found in the hills in 1868. Settlers called the valley Parley's Park, and the town took the shortened name when it incorporated in 1884. The mines made a handful of fortunes, then faded, and the town shrank badly before skiing brought it back in the 1980s and 1990s ([history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_City,_Utah)). You can still see the mining era everywhere: old mine buildings on the ski slopes, and a 19th-century Main Street of wooden storefronts climbing the hill.

### An Olympic town, twice

Park City was the mountain half of the 2002 Salt Lake City Winter Olympics — more than 40% of the events were held here, including a third of all medal events ([Visit Park City](https://www.visitparkcity.com/about-park-city/olympics/)). The Games come back to Utah in 2034 using the same venues, and Olympic athletes still train here, so you can watch bobsledders and ski jumpers practice at the Olympic Park.

### Two resorts, and the one we'll use

**Park City Mountain** is the largest ski resort in the United States, about 7,300 acres. Its main base sits at the edge of Old Town, a few blocks from Main Street, with the sleigh-ride dinner and the Alpine Coaster at the same base. Its group ski school covers **every age in our group**: kids 7–14 and adults 15+ — that's why it's the pick for this trip.

**Deer Valley** is the polished, ski-only resort next door, with an excellent ski school. It doesn't fit us as well: its teen group lessons don't run on our ski days, and adults' lessons and tickets cost more.

### Why Park City over Colorado

- **Shortest trip.** One nonstop flight from New Orleans, then about 40 minutes on an interstate.
- **Lower altitude.** The town sits around 7,000 ft. Colorado's Summit County bases are closer to 9,500 ft.
- **The most to do off the slopes,** all within about 30 minutes — matters most on a trip that's mainly about group fun.
- **Mardi Gras week is quiet out here.** Most of the country is in school Feb 5–12, and we leave before Presidents' Day weekend (Feb 13–15).

### Honest downsides

- **It's expensive.** A peak-day adult lift ticket at the window is $369 at Park City Mountain this season ([TownLift](https://townlift.com/2026/09/park-city-deer-valley-among-the-countrys-priciest-ski-days-as-2026-27-tickets-climb-again/)). Buying in advance avoids most of that.
- **The canyon drive can close.** I-80 up from Salt Lake gets snow-packed in storms, and Utah's traction law can require AWD. Leave buffer on airport days.
- **Altitude is real.** Expect headaches, dry noses and poor sleep the first night or two — one reason the ski days come later in the week.

### Bad-weather plan

| If this happens                            | Do this                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| The canyon is closed or slow when you land | Don't fight it. Eat in Salt Lake, or spend two hours at the Natural History Museum of Utah (dinosaurs, indoors, near the canyon mouth), and check [UDOT Traffic](https://udottraffic.utah.gov). Closures usually clear within hours. |
| It snows on a ski day                      | Ski anyway. Lessons run in falling snow, and fresh snow is softer to fall on. Only high wind or lightning stops lifts.                                                                                                               |
| A big storm or high winds on Mon–Tue       | Swap the ski days with Wed–Thu, and move the Midway day earlier. Park City Mountain lessons can be changed without penalty 48 hours or more ahead.                                                                                   |
| Too cold or too tired for outdoor plans    | Indoor backups: the Olympic Park museums, Swaner's exhibits, the Ice Arena, Woodward's indoor trampolines and skate park, or a Salt Lake museum day.                                                                                 |
| A low-snow winter                          | The beginner areas and tubing hills rely on snowmaking, so lessons and tubing still happen. Watch the snow reports in January. Dog sledding and the Ice Castles are the activities most at risk.                                     |
| Thin snow cancels dog sledding             | Luna Lobos switches to a wheeled "dry land" sled ride. Deposits aren't refundable, so go anyway.                                                                                                                                     |
| Storm on departure day                     | Leave for the airport early, by at least an extra hour. Check UDOT first. If flights cancel, the insurance's travel-delay coverage helps with the extra night.                                                                       |

### Flights

**Delta, New Orleans to Salt Lake City, nonstop** on [delta.com](https://www.delta.com). Delta flies the route once a day ([MSY airport](https://flymsy.com/delta-airlines/)), and the flight takes about 3 hours 40 minutes. Everything else connects, which adds hours and a missed-connection risk in February weather.

**Estimate:** $500–750 round trip per person, so $4,000–6,000 for eight. Book all eight seats on the same flight, and check the nonstop's departure time before planning day one — if it lands after dark, do the canyon drive and groceries, and save sledding for Saturday morning.

**Checked bags:** Delta charges about $45 for the first bag and $55 for the second, each way, unless someone holds a Delta credit card with free bags. Winter clothes take a lot of space, so **wear the bulkiest layers on the plane** (boots, jacket, fleece) and pack the rest in compression bags.

**ID:** every adult needs a **REAL ID** driver's license (the one with a star) or a passport to fly. Kids under 18 don't need ID on domestic flights.

<Note label="Utah's traction law">
  During storms, UDOT can require proper traction on I-80 through Parleys Canyon and in Park City
  ([UDOT](https://wasatchback.udot.utah.gov/traction-law/)). AWD vehicles need M+S or
  snowflake-rated tires on all four wheels. Enterprise, Alamo and National let you reserve a
  **"Guaranteed AWD"** class, so book that rather than "SUV or similar".
</Note>

### Booster seat for the 7-year-old

Utah requires a car seat or booster for children under 8, unless they're taller than 57 inches. The 7-year-old needs a booster in the SUV — the easiest option is a **backless booster from home**, small and light enough to carry on the plane. Rental companies rent them too, usually for a daily fee. The 10 and 14-year-olds can use regular seat belts.

### In town: free buses

Park City's buses are free every day and connect Old Town, Main Street, both resorts and Kimball Junction. On the ski days and the Main Street evening, leave the cars parked.

### Lift access

- **Both 10-year-olds:** [Ski Utah Passport](https://www.skiutah.com/passes/passports/fifth-grade-passport-faq/), $79 each if bought by Nov 30 ([TownLift](https://townlift.com/2026/06/ski-utah-opens-2026-27-passport-program-for-young-skiers-and-snowboarders/)). Covers 3 days at each of Utah's 15 resorts, including Park City Mountain. Requires proof of 4th–6th grade; no Utah residency needed.
- **Adults, the 14-year-old and the 7-year-old:** [Epic 2-Day Pass](https://www.epicpass.com), bought before early December. Park City Mountain starts at $98/day this way ([Park Record](https://www.parkrecord.com/?p=275003)), against $369 at the window on peak days. Estimate $200–280 per adult or teen for 2 days. The 7-year-old pays the cheaper child rate (ages 5–12), roughly half.
- **Or:** add the discounted lift ticket when booking the lesson, and compare that price with the Epic 2-Day Pass.

<Note type="win" label="Why this works for a fun-first trip">
  Full-day group lessons on back-to-back days take every first-timer from "never stood on skis" to
  riding a chairlift down an easy green run. Park City Mountain also has a **Green Run Guarantee**:
  if a first-timer in full-day group lessons hasn't reached green runs by the end of the third
  lesson, the fourth is free. With two days we won't use it, but it signals how the program is
  built.
</Note>

### Ski-day logistics

- **A parent has to be there at kids' drop-off and pick-up.** With all four adults in lessons too, ask for the kids' check-in time and the adult lesson start time when booking, and drop the kids first.
- **Book on the Park City side, not Canyons,** so lessons meet at the base you're walking to.
- **Get there early.** Boots, helmets and goggles take longer than you expect with four kids — aim to arrive 45 minutes before check-in on day one.
- **The 14-year-old will be in the 7–14 group,** grouped by ability and roughly by age. Tell them ahead of time so it's not a surprise to be with younger kids.
- **Each kid carries:** a parent's phone number in a pocket, a small snack, lip balm and sunscreen. Lunch is included on full-day lessons; adults buy or bring their own, and an early lunch (before 11:30) beats the noon crowds.
- **Tip the instructors** at pick-up ([section 14](#basics)).

Day one is on the flat learning area and the conveyor ("magic carpet") lifts: putting skis on, gliding, stopping in a wedge. Day two usually gets kids onto a chairlift and an easy green run. Adults often learn more slowly than kids, which is normal — plan to finish day two with a group run together, then the Alpine Coaster.

Deer Valley is the alternative if you want its ski-only slopes and smaller classes, but its adult groups are 18+ and its teen groups don't run Feb 8–9, so the 14-year-old would need a private lesson, and adult tickets cost more.

<Section id="insure" num="11" title="Travel insurance" subtitle="For a domestic trip, insurance is mostly about protecting the money you prepay. This trip has one specific risk that standard policies probably won't cover.">

<div class="measure">

### The specific risk: make-up days

The Jefferson Parish calendar marks the Mardi Gras break days as possible make-up days for emergencies or bad weather. If a hurricane or storm closes schools in the fall, the district could pull kids back into class during your trip.

**Standard trip cancellation probably won't pay for that.** It covers a fixed list of reasons — illness, injury, a death in the family, severe weather that disrupts travel. Some plans cover a school extending its year into your travel dates when attendance is required ([Generali](https://www.generalitravelinsurance.com/travel-resources/coverage-for-school.html)), but make-up days inside a scheduled break may not count. Don't rely on it unless the plan document says so explicitly.

### What does cover it: Cancel For Any Reason

A **Cancel For Any Reason (CFAR)** upgrade pays out no matter why you cancel ([Squaremouth](https://www.squaremouth.com/current-event-information-centers/faqs/what-is-cancel-for-any-reason-coverage)):

- **It reimburses up to 75%** of your trip cost, depending on the plan. Many plans pay 50–75%.
- **You must buy it within 14–30 days of your first trip payment.** With flights and lodging going on cards this month, that window opens soon.
- **You must insure 100% of your prepaid, non-refundable costs.** Refundable bookings don't need to be included.
- **You must cancel the whole trip at least 2–3 days before departure.**

### What to look for

- **CFAR upgrade available,** at the highest percentage you can find
- **Snow sports covered as recreation.** Some plans exclude skiing or charge extra — lessons and green runs should count as amateur recreation, but read the exclusions.
- **Trip interruption,** in case someone gets hurt mid-week and you fly home early
- **Travel delay and missed connection,** for a canyon or airport snow closure
- **Medical and evacuation** are less critical in the US, since your health insurance works here, but evacuation coverage still helps for a mountain injury

### How to buy it

- **Each family buys its own policy,** covering only its own prepaid costs.
- **Compare plans on [Squaremouth](https://www.squaremouth.com)**, a comparison site with a "Cancel For Any Reason" filter.
- **Estimate:** $400–900 per family with CFAR, assuming ~$8,000–10,000 of non-refundable costs per family.

<Note type="win" label="Recommendation">
  Buy a CFAR policy through Squaremouth on the same day you pay your first deposit. Also lower the
  amount at risk: book **Delta Main Cabin, not Basic Economy**, so a cancelled flight becomes a
  credit instead of a loss, and choose lodging with the most flexible cancellation policy you can
  get.
</Note>

</div>

</Section>

<Section id="pack" num="12" title="What to buy and pack" subtitle="This guide assumes neither family owns snow gear, so most of this is a shopping list: a layering system that works for every activity, buy only what you can't rent, and don't overspend on kids' sizes they'll outgrow by next winter.">

### The layering system

February in Park City usually means daytime highs around freezing and nights well below. The cold is dry and the sun is strong, so you'll get hot climbing a tubing hill and cold sitting in a sleigh. Everyone, every day, wears three layers:

- **Base layer,** top and bottom, against the skin. Synthetic or merino, **never cotton** — cotton holds sweat and gets cold.
- **Mid layer:** a fleece or a light puffy jacket for warmth. Take it off when you're working hard.
- **Outer layer:** a waterproof jacket and **snow pants**. Snow pants matter more than you'd think — tubing, sledding and falling on skis all mean sitting in snow.

Add mittens, a neck gaiter, goggles, a helmet on ski days, and warm boots, and that one outfit covers the whole week.

### Utah foods to try

- **Fry sauce** — Utah's signature dip (ketchup and mayo, sometimes with barbecue sauce), served with fries almost everywhere. Arctic Circle is the classic chain.
- **Dirty soda** — soda mixed with cream, flavored syrups and fruit. Swig and Sodalicious are the big chains, both on the Salt Lake side of the airport drive.
- **Utah scones** — not a British scone. Fried bread dough, puffy and hot, served with honey butter.
- **Funeral potatoes** — a cheesy potato casserole with a crunchy topping, named for the church potluck meals served after funerals.
- **Green Jell-O** — Utah's official state snack since 2001, a running local joke.
- **Pastrami burger** — a cheeseburger piled with pastrami, a Salt Lake specialty at Crown Burgers.

### A Main Street walk

Historic Main Street climbs a steep hill. Ride the free bus to the top and walk down, so the kids aren't climbing — allow 1–2 hours with the museum. Highlights: the **Top of Main Brew Pub** (Utah's first, open since 1986), the **Egyptian Theatre**, the **No Name Saloon**, the **Park City Museum** at 528 Main with the 1885 jail cells still in the basement, historic mine-claim plaques to hunt for, a dozen or more art galleries, and the **Town Lift** at the bottom carrying skiers straight onto Park City Mountain.

### Utah's alcohol rules (for the adults)

Grocery and convenience stores sell beer up to 5% ABV and nothing stronger. Wine, spirits and stronger beer come from state liquor stores, **closed on Sundays**, generally open 11 a.m.–5–10 p.m. Mon–Sat — stock up on Saturday. Restaurants serve drinks only with food; brewpubs can serve beer without it. Bringing alcohol into Utah is technically illegal, so just buy it there. Utah's DUI limit is 0.05%, lower than Louisiana's 0.08% — plan who's driving on any night out.

### Good to know

- **Park City isn't typical Utah.** Most of the state has a strong Latter-day Saint (Mormon) culture. Park City grew up as a rowdy mining town full of saloons, and it's still more of a resort town than the valleys around it.
- **Moose sometimes wander through town** in winter, especially near trails and creeks. They look calm but can charge — give them lots of space and keep kids well back.
- **The sun is stronger than it feels.** At 7,000 ft with snow reflecting light, kids burn on cloudy days — sunscreen every morning.
- **It's one hour behind New Orleans** (Mountain Time). Kids will wake early the first morning, which works fine for a snow day.
- **Dress is casual everywhere,** including the nice restaurants. Snow boots and fleece are normal at dinner.

### Altitude and kids

The town is about 7,000 ft, and the slopes go higher. Coming from sea level, many people feel some effects in the first day or two: headache, tiredness, a poor first night's sleep, nosebleeds from the dry air. The plan puts two easy days before skiing partly for this. This is general travel advice, not medical advice — ask your pediatrician if a child has asthma or another condition.

- **Water, constantly.** Dry air plus exertion dehydrates kids fast.
- **Watch the 7-year-old.** Crankiness and not eating can be the first signs of altitude trouble.
- **Get help if symptoms get worse** instead of better, especially vomiting, confusion, or trouble breathing.
- A humidifier or a bowl of water in the bedrooms helps with dry noses and sleep.
